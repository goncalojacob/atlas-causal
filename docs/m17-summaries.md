## afonso-costa

`actor`

**Before**

> Jurist and leader of the Democratic Party, the dominant figure of the First Republic: author of the 1911 separation of church and state, several times head of government, and the principal advocate of Portuguese belligerence in the First World War. He left for Paris after 1917 and never governed again.

**After**

> Jurist, professor of law and the dominant politician of the First Republic, Afonso Costa (1871–1937) led the Democratic Party, the largest of the republican parties, and headed the government three times between 1913 and 1917. He drafted the law separating church and state in 1911, put the state's finances briefly in order, and was the principal advocate of Portuguese belligerence in the First World War, which he defended as the price of keeping the colonies and a seat at the peace. The war divided the republic and cost him his position: he was deposed by Sidónio Pais in December 1917, and after leading the delegation to the Paris peace conference he settled in Paris and never governed again. Historians differ over whether his anticlericalism and his party's methods deepened the divisions the republic fell to, or whether he was the only figure capable of governing it at all. He is in the atlas as the actor at the centre of the republic's decisions between 1910 and 1917.

## alvaro-cunhal

`actor`

**Before**

> Secretary-general of the Portuguese Communist Party from 1961 to 1992, imprisoned for eleven years and in exile until 1974. The party he led was the best-organised force of the revolutionary period and the pivot of the disputes of 1975.

**After**

> Álvaro Cunhal (1913–2005) was secretary-general of the Portuguese Communist Party from 1961 to 1992, after eleven years in prison — eight of them in solitary confinement — an escape from Peniche in 1960 and fourteen years of exile. He returned to Portugal on 30 April 1974 and served as a minister without portfolio in the provisional governments. The party he had rebuilt clandestinely was the best-organised political force of the revolutionary period, with roots in the Alentejo and in the industrial belt, and it stood at the centre of the disputes of 1975 over the direction of the revolution and the place of elections in it. Whether the party was attempting to take power in 1975 or defending gains it expected to lose is argued about to this day. He is in the atlas as one of the two poles — Soares the other — around which the politics of 1975 organised itself.

## amilcar-cabral

`actor`

**Before**

> Agronomist and founder of the PAIGC, the strategist of the liberation war in Guinea and the most internationally effective of the leaders of the Portuguese-African movements. He was assassinated in Conakry in January 1973, months before Guinea-Bissau declared independence.

**After**

> Amílcar Cabral (1924–1973), an agronomist trained in Lisbon, founded the PAIGC in 1956 and led the liberation war in Portuguese Guinea and Cape Verde from 1963. He was the movement's strategist and its theorist, and the most internationally effective of the leaders of the Portuguese-African movements: the PAIGC combined guerrilla war with administration, schools and clinics in the areas it held, and won diplomatic recognition at the United Nations before it had a state. He was assassinated outside his house in Conakry in January 1973 by members of his own movement, eight months before Guinea-Bissau declared independence. He is in the atlas as the actor whose work made the Guinea front the one Portugal came closest to losing outright.

## antonio-de-spinola

`actor`

**Before**

> General and former governor of Guinea whose book "Portugal e o Futuro" broke with the regime's colonial policy in February 1974. He became the first president after the revolution, resigned in September 1974, and after a failed coup in March 1975 went into exile.

**After**

> António de Spínola (1910–1996), a cavalry general who had governed Portuguese Guinea and commanded there from 1968 to 1973, published Portugal e o Futuro in February 1974 arguing that the colonial war had no military solution. Dismissed with Costa Gomes in March, he was accepted by the Armed Forces Movement as the public face of the 25 April coup and became the first president of the new republic. He wanted a federal solution for the African territories and a slower, more presidential transition; the movement wanted independence and a faster one. He resigned after the confrontation of 28 September 1974, attempted to reverse the course on 11 March 1975, and went into exile. He is in the atlas because two of the period's turning points are his defeats.

## cavaco-silva

`actor`

**Before**

> Economist and leader of the Social Democratic Party, prime minister from 1985 to 1995 with absolute majorities from 1987, and president from 2006 to 2016. His governments coincided with accession to the European Community and the largest inflow of structural funds.

**After**

> Aníbal Cavaco Silva (born 1939), an economist, led the Social Democratic Party from 1985 and was prime minister from that year until 1995, with absolute majorities from 1987 — the first stable single-party government of the Third Republic. His governments coincided with accession to the European Community, the largest inflow of structural funds the country had received, the constitutional revision of 1989 that allowed the nationalised sector to be sold, and the privatisations that followed. He was President of the Republic from 2006 to 2016, and so held that office through the crisis of 2011 and the adjustment programme. He is in the atlas as the actor who connects accession to Europe with the economic settlement that the arguments about 2011 are conducted over.

## estado-novo

`actor`

**Before**

> The corporatist authoritarian regime founded on the 1933 constitution, with a single party, a political police and censorship, which held the African territories by war from 1961 and fell on 25 April 1974.

**After**

> The Estado Novo was the authoritarian regime founded on the constitution of 1933 and dissolved on 25 April 1974, the longest-lived of its kind in twentieth-century Europe. It was corporatist in doctrine and Catholic and conservative in temper, with a single party, a political police, censorship, a corporative chamber and no free elections; Salazar governed it until 1968 and Marcelo Caetano until its fall. It stayed out of the world war while granting the Azores to the Allies, joined NATO in 1949 and the United Nations in 1955, and from 1961 held the African territories by a war on three fronts that consumed nearly half the state budget. Historians disagree over how far to call it fascist and how much of it was improvised around one man; they agree that the war is what ended it. It is in the atlas as the polity most of the events between 1933 and 1974 belong to.

## first-portuguese-republic

`actor`

**Before**

> The parliamentary republic proclaimed on 5 October 1910 and ended by the coup of 28 May 1926. Sixteen years of chronic instability: dozens of governments, recurrent revolts, an unpopular war, and a monarchist and then a military reaction.

**After**

> The First Portuguese Republic was proclaimed on 5 October 1910 and ended by the coup of 28 May 1926. In sixteen years it had more than forty governments and eight presidents, and lived through the separation of church and state, a divisive entry into the First World War, monarchist risings, the presidential interlude of Sidónio Pais and repeated military interventions. It also brought universal male suffrage in principle, a free press for most of the period, and the first serious attempt at mass schooling. Its failure is argued about in terms of the constitution, of the parties, and of a country that was largely rural, poor and unpersuaded of the republic. It is in the atlas as the polity from which both the dictatorship and the memory that justified it descend.

## henrique-galvao

`actor`

**Before**

> Officer and colonial administrator who turned against the regime after reporting on forced labour in Angola, escaped from custody, and in January 1961 led the seizure of the liner Santa Maria to draw world attention to the dictatorship.

**After**

> Henrique Galvão (1895–1970) was an officer, colonial administrator and writer who turned against the Estado Novo after a 1947 report to the National Assembly described forced labour in Angola in terms the regime would not accept. Imprisoned, he escaped from custody in 1959 and took refuge in Latin America. In January 1961 he led the seizure of the liner Santa Maria in the Caribbean, holding it for eleven days to force the world's press to attend to the dictatorship — an operation that succeeded as publicity and coincided, weeks later, with the outbreak of the war in Angola. He is in the atlas as the clearest case of opposition from inside the colonial administration itself.

## humberto-delgado

`actor`

**Before**

> Air force general who stood against the regime in the presidential election of 1958 and turned it into the widest legal opposition mobilisation the Estado Novo faced. Exiled afterwards, he was lured to the Spanish border and murdered by the PIDE in 1965.

**After**

> Humberto Delgado (1906–1965) was an air force general who had served the regime — he helped negotiate the Azores agreements and represented Portugal at NATO — before standing against it in the presidential election of June 1958. His campaign drew the largest crowds the opposition had assembled, and his promise to dismiss Salazar made him the focus of everything the regime had suppressed; the official result gave him about a quarter of the vote and was widely disbelieved. Dismissed and exiled, he was involved in the Beja barracks attempt of 1962 and in other conspiracies, and in February 1965 he was lured to the Spanish border and murdered by the political police, his body found near Villanueva del Fresno. He is in the atlas as the man whose candidacy cost the regime the last of its electoral forms.

## imf

`actor`

**Before**

> The fund whose stabilisation programmes framed Portuguese economic policy after the balance-of-payments crises of 1977 and 1983, and again, with the European institutions, in 2011.

**After**

> The International Monetary Fund, founded in 1945, appears in this atlas as one of the external constraints on Portuguese economic policy. Its stabilisation programmes framed the response to the balance-of-payments crises of 1977 and 1983 that followed the revolution and the oil shocks, and it returned in 2011 as one of the three institutions — with the European Commission and the European Central Bank — behind the adjustment programme. It is in the atlas because twice, a generation apart, the terms of Portuguese fiscal policy were written with it at the table.

## indonesia

`actor`

**Before**

> Independent from the Netherlands after 1945; it invaded Portuguese Timor in December 1975 and occupied it until 1999, when a United Nations ballot led to withdrawal.

**After**

> The Republic of Indonesia, independent from the Netherlands after 1945, invaded Portuguese Timor in December 1975, months after Portugal's administration collapsed there, and annexed it the following year. It occupied the territory until 1999, when it accepted a United Nations ballot on autonomy; the vote went against it, and its withdrawal was preceded by militia violence that destroyed much of the territory. Only a handful of states recognised the annexation, and Portugal never did. It is in the atlas as the actor that took the last territory Portugal had failed to decolonise.

## jawaharlal-nehru

`actor`

**Before**

> First prime minister of independent India, from 1947 until his death. His government took Goa, Daman and Diu by force in December 1961 after years of failed negotiation with Lisbon.

**After**

> Jawaharlal Nehru (1889–1964) was the first prime minister of independent India, in office from 1947 until his death. After years in which Lisbon refused to negotiate over the enclaves on India's coast — Salazar held that Goa was not a colony but Portugal — and after economic blockade and the shooting of unarmed satyagrahis in 1955, his government took Goa, Daman and Diu by force in December 1961. The action was popular in India and defended there as decolonisation; abroad it cost Nehru some of his standing as an advocate of non-violence. He is in the atlas as the actor who ended Portuguese rule in India.

## legiao-portuguesa

`actor`

**Before**

> The regime's militia, founded in 1936 in the atmosphere of the Spanish Civil War as an anti-communist paramilitary body; it survived, with declining importance, until 1974.

**After**

> The Portuguese Legion was the regime's volunteer militia, created in September 1936 in the atmosphere of the Spanish Civil War as an anti-communist paramilitary body and as a counterweight, inside the regime, to the army. It ran civil defence and later the state's radio-monitoring service, took part in surveillance alongside the political police, and provided the regime with the parades and uniforms of the period. Its importance declined after the world war, though it survived on paper until it was dissolved after 25 April 1974. It is in the atlas as the militia arm of the Estado Novo, beside the political police.

## manuel-ii

`actor`

**Before**

> The last king of Portugal, on the throne from the assassination of his father and brother in 1908 until the republican revolution of 5 October 1910, after which he lived in exile in England until his death.

**After**

> Manuel II (1889–1932) came to the throne on 1 February 1908, when his father Carlos I and his elder brother were shot dead in the Terreiro do Paço, and reigned until the republican revolution of 5 October 1910. His two and a half years were spent trying to govern through rotating ministries in a monarchy whose credit had been spent by the dictatorship of João Franco. He left for exile in England, refused to endorse the monarchist risings made in his name during the First Republic, and died at Twickenham in 1932. He is in the atlas as the last king, and as the endpoint of the chain that begins the twentieth century in Portugal.

## marcelo-caetano

`actor`

**Before**

> Jurist and long-serving figure of the regime who succeeded Salazar as president of the council in 1968. His "evolution in continuity" liberalised at the margins without ending the colonial war, and he was deposed on 25 April 1974 and exiled to Brazil.

**After**

> Marcelo Caetano (1906–1980) was a professor of administrative law who had spent his career inside the regime — youth organisation, colonial ministry, presidency of the council's executive committee — before succeeding Salazar as President of the Council in September 1968. His "evolution in continuity" relaxed censorship and the political police a little, widened the electorate, and let a reformist wing into the National Assembly; it did not end the colonial war, legalise opposition parties, or survive the pressure of the two. By 1972 the reformists had gone and the regime was governing as before. He surrendered at the Carmo barracks on 25 April 1974 and died in exile in Brazil. He is in the atlas as the actor whose failure to reform the regime is what made its fall a coup rather than a transition.

## mario-soares

`actor`

**Before**

> Socialist leader, repeatedly imprisoned and then deported under the Estado Novo. Foreign minister and prime minister after 1974, he negotiated the decolonisation agreements and the accession to the European Community, and was president from 1986 to 1996.

**After**

> Mário Soares (1924–2017), a lawyer who had defended political prisoners and been imprisoned and deported to São Tomé under the Estado Novo, founded the Socialist Party in exile in 1973 and returned to Portugal three days after the 1974 coup. As foreign minister he signed the agreements that decolonised the African territories; as prime minister from 1976 he governed a minority government, applied to the European Community in 1977 and accepted the first IMF programme. He was the principal opponent of the revolutionary course in 1975 and, from 1986 to 1996, the first civilian president in sixty years. His reputation rests on holding a parliamentary line at a moment when it was not obviously going to hold; his critics then and since argue about the cost of the decolonisation agreements he negotiated. He is in the atlas as one of the two poles of the politics of 1975 and the figure who carries the new republic into Europe.

## military-dictatorship

`actor`

**Before**

> The regime installed by the coup of 28 May 1926, which governed without a constitution while its internal disputes were settled, and out of which the Estado Novo was built once Salazar had taken control of the finances.

**After**

> The Military Dictatorship was the regime installed by the coup of 28 May 1926 and superseded by the constitution of 1933. It governed without a constitution and without a programme beyond ending the parliamentary republic, and spent its first two years in disputes among its own factions: Gomes da Costa was removed within weeks, Óscar Carmona became president, and revolts against the dictatorship in 1927 and 1931 were put down. Its unsolved problem was the deficit, which is what brought Salazar into the finance ministry in 1928 and, from there, into the presidency of the council in 1932. It is in the atlas as the interval in which the Estado Novo was assembled, and it is deliberately a separate actor from the regime that came out of it.

## nato

`actor`

**Before**

> The Atlantic alliance founded in 1949, of which Portugal was a founding member despite being a dictatorship — a membership that shaped both its armed forces and its standing among the Western powers during the colonial war.

**After**

> The North Atlantic Treaty Organization was founded in April 1949, with Portugal among the twelve original signatories and the only one that was not a democracy — a fact explained by the Azores and by what the 1943 agreement had shown they were worth. Membership professionalised the Portuguese officer corps, sent officers to train abroad, and equipped an army that from 1961 was used for a purpose the alliance had not intended; other members' discomfort about that grew through the 1960s without producing much action. It is in the atlas because the alliance shaped both the armed forces that fought the colonial war and the officers who ended it.

## otelo-saraiva-de-carvalho

`actor`

**Before**

> Officer who drew up the operational plan of the 25 April 1974 coup and commanded COPCON during the revolutionary period, becoming the emblem of its most radical wing.

**After**

> Otelo Saraiva de Carvalho (1936–2021) was the officer who drew up the operational plan of the 25 April 1974 coup, having served in Angola and Guinea, and who then commanded COPCON, the operational command created in July 1974 that policed the revolutionary period. He became the emblem of its most radical wing, and lost his command after 25 November 1975. He stood twice for president, in 1976 and 1980, and in the 1980s was tried and convicted in connection with the FP-25 armed group — a conviction later annulled. He is in the atlas as the actor at the centre of both the coup and the confrontation that ended the period the coup opened.

## partido-democratico

`actor`

**Before**

> The largest party of the First Republic, heir to the Portuguese Republican Party and led by Afonso Costa; in government for most of the period, and the target of every reaction against it.

**After**

> The Democratic Party was the largest party of the First Republic, the wing of the old Portuguese Republican Party that kept its name and machine after the 1912 split, and it was led by Afonso Costa. It was in government for most of the republic's sixteen years, controlled its electoral apparatus, and drove the war policy and the anticlerical legislation. That dominance made it the target of every reaction — the Pimenta de Castro government, the Sidónio Pais coup, and finally 28 May 1926, after which it was dissolved with the rest. It is in the atlas as the party through which most republican decisions were taken, and against which most of the period's revolts were made.

## portuguese-expeditionary-corps

`actor`

**Before**

> The force sent to Flanders after Germany declared war on Portugal in 1916. Badly supplied and left too long in the line, it was broken at the Lys in April 1918, a defeat that marked the army's politics for a decade.

**After**

> The Portuguese Expeditionary Corps was the force sent to Flanders from January 1917 after Germany's declaration of war, some 55,000 men at its height, holding a sector of the British line under British command. It was poorly supplied, and after the fall of Afonso Costa's government in December 1917 the reinforcement and rotation it depended on effectively stopped; its divisions were left too long in the line. The 2nd Division was broken by the German attack on the Lys on 9 April 1918 and the corps was withdrawn from front-line duty. The officers who returned brought a grievance against the republic's politicians that shaped the army's politics for a decade. It is in the atlas as the actor whose experience links the war policy of 1916 to the coup of 1926.

## pvde-pide-dgs

`actor`

**Before**

> The political police of the Estado Novo, under three names in succession, responsible for surveillance, the political prisons and, in cases such as the killing of Humberto Delgado, for murder. Dissolved after 25 April 1974.

**After**

> The political police of the Estado Novo ran under three names in succession — PVDE from 1933, PIDE from 1945, DGS from 1969 — and is one actor here because it was one institution throughout. It ran surveillance, informers and censorship of correspondence, held prisoners at Caxias, Peniche and, until 1954, the camp at Tarrafal in Cape Verde, and used torture in interrogation; in the colonies it operated alongside the war. It murdered Humberto Delgado in 1965, and its involvement in the death of Amílcar Cabral in 1973 has been argued about ever since. It was dissolved after 25 April 1974, and its archive is a principal source for the period. It is in the atlas as the instrument through which the regime dealt with what it could not govern by consent.

## ramalho-eanes

`actor`

**Before**

> Officer who commanded the operation that ended the revolutionary period on 25 November 1975 and was elected president in 1976, serving two terms and presiding over the return of the military to barracks.

**After**

> Ramalho Eanes (born 1935), an officer who had served in India, Angola, Mozambique and Guinea, commanded the operation that put down the events of 25 November 1975 and was elected President of the Republic in June 1976 with the support of the Socialists, the Social Democrats and the CDS. He was re-elected in 1980 and served until 1986, presiding over the return of the armed forces to their barracks, the constitutional revision of 1982 that abolished the Council of the Revolution he had chaired, and a series of unstable governments. He is in the atlas as the actor who closes the revolutionary period and then dismantles the institutions it had left behind.

## republic-of-india

`actor`

**Before**

> Independent from 1947, and from the beginning a claimant to the Portuguese enclaves on its coast. After years of negotiation and blockade it annexed Goa, Daman and Diu by force in December 1961.

**After**

> The Republic of India, independent from 1947, claimed the Portuguese territories on its coast — Goa, Daman, Diu, and the enclaves of Dadra and Nagar Haveli — from the beginning, and Portugal refused to negotiate on the ground that they were not colonies but part of Portugal. Diplomatic relations were broken in 1955, the enclaves were taken by volunteers the same year, and after years of blockade Indian forces occupied Goa, Daman and Diu in December 1961. Portugal recognised the loss only in 1974. It is in the atlas as the actor that ended Portuguese rule in Asia's mainland, and whose action hardened Lisbon's determination to hold Africa.

## salazar

`actor`

**Before**

> Coimbra professor of finance who became minister of finance in 1928 and president of the council from 1932 to 1968, and who built and ran the Estado Novo. Incapacitated by a stroke in 1968, he was replaced without being told.

**After**

> António de Oliveira Salazar (1889–1970), a professor of finance at Coimbra, entered the Military Dictatorship's government as finance minister in 1928 with a veto over all public spending, became President of the Council in 1932, and wrote the constitution that founded the Estado Novo in 1933. He governed for thirty-six years: austere public finances, corporatism, censorship, a political police, no legal opposition, neutrality in the world war combined with the Azores concession to the Allies, NATO in 1949, and from 1961 a refusal to decolonise that committed the country to war on three fronts. Historians differ over whether the regime is best described as fascist or as conservative Catholic authoritarianism, and over how much of its longevity was his own doing. Incapacitated in 1968, he was replaced without being informed and died in 1970 believing he still governed. He is in the atlas as the actor most of the twentieth-century Portuguese chains pass through.

## sidonio-pais

`actor`

**Before**

> Army officer and professor who seized power in December 1917 and governed as a plebiscitary president until he was shot dead in Lisbon in December 1918. His "New Republic" is read by some historians as a first Portuguese experiment with charismatic authoritarian rule.

**After**

> Sidónio Pais (1872–1918), an army officer and professor of mathematics who had been a minister and then Portugal's envoy in Berlin, overthrew the Democratic government in December 1917 and governed as head of a junta and then as a directly elected president, with the parties displaced and parliament reduced. His "New Republic" restored relations with the Church, courted Catholic and monarchist support, and drew on a personal popularity that the republic's parties had never had; it also arrested opponents and did not survive its author. He was shot dead at Rossio station in December 1918. Some historians read the episode as a first Portuguese experiment with charismatic authoritarian rule and a precedent later regimes drew on; others treat it as a personal government with no doctrine behind it. He is in the atlas as the republic's one attempt to be governed by one man before 1926.

## third-portuguese-republic

`actor`

**Before**

> The democratic regime opened by the revolution of 25 April 1974 and given its form by the constitution of 1976: parliamentary, with a directly elected president, in the European Community from 1986.

**After**

> The Third Portuguese Republic is the regime opened by the revolution of 25 April 1974 and given its form by the constitution of 2 April 1976: a parliamentary republic with a directly elected president, and, until the revision of 1982, a Council of the Revolution of officers beside them. Its first years were the decolonisation of the African territories, the return of some half a million people from them, two IMF programmes and a series of short governments; from 1986 it is a member of the European Community, from 1999 of the euro, and in 2011 the subject of an adjustment programme. It is the polity that every event in this atlas after 1974 belongs to.

## united-nations

`actor`

**Before**

> Portugal was kept out until 1955 and, once admitted, faced sustained pressure over the African territories, which it answered by reclassifying them as overseas provinces.

**After**

> The United Nations kept Portugal out until 1955, when a bloc-to-bloc package unblocked sixteen admissions at once; from the end of the 1950s the organisation became the principal forum of pressure over the African territories. Portugal answered by renaming them overseas provinces and denying that the obligation to report on non-self-governing territories applied, an argument the General Assembly rejected in 1960; sanctions and resolutions followed through the war years without Portugal's NATO allies enforcing much of them. The organisation later administered East Timor between the 1999 ballot and independence in 2002. It is in the atlas as the arena in which the legitimacy of Portuguese rule in Africa was contested.

## vasco-goncalves

`actor`

**Before**

> Officer of the Armed Forces Movement and prime minister through most of 1974 and 1975, over five provisional governments; his removal in the summer of 1975 marked the turn away from the revolutionary course.

**After**

> Vasco Gonçalves (1921–2005) was an officer of the Armed Forces Movement who became prime minister in July 1974 and led five of the six provisional governments, through the nationalisations, the agrarian reform and the confrontations of 1975. He was identified with the movement's left and with cooperation with the Communist Party, and his removal in September 1975, after the moderate officers' Document of the Nine and weeks of pressure, marked the turn away from the revolutionary course two months before 25 November. He is in the atlas as the head of government through the year in which the shape of the new state was fought over.

## 25-november-1975

`event`

**Before**

> The failed radical military uprising of 25 November 1975 and its suppression by the moderate wing of the army ended the revolutionary period.

**After**

> Paratroopers from the bases north of Lisbon seized several air force units on 25 November 1975; the moderate wing of the Armed Forces Movement read the seizures as an attempted left-wing coup, while parts of the left described them as a reaction to the removal of radical officers already under way. The operation against them, commanded by Ramalho Eanes from a new operational command, was over within a day and cost few lives. Otelo Saraiva de Carvalho lost COPCON and the officers around him lost their commands, and the Communist Party, which had not moved, was left isolated. Historians still disagree about how far any coordinated left-wing plan existed; they do not disagree about the result, which was that the constitution then being written would be applied by a parliamentary republic and not by a revolutionary one. It is in the atlas as the endpoint where the several branches of 1975 converge.

## alvor-agreement-1975

`event`

**Before**

> Portugal, the MPLA, the FNLA and UNITA agreed at Alvor in January 1975 on a transitional government and independence for Angola on 11 November. The agreement collapsed within months into war between the three movements.

**After**

> Portugal and the three Angolan liberation movements — the MPLA, the FNLA and UNITA — signed the Alvor agreement in the Algarve on 15 January 1975, with Mário Soares negotiating for Lisbon. It set up a transitional government of all four parties, a joint army, and independence for Angola on 11 November 1975. The transitional government never worked: fighting between the movements in Luanda from March, and the foreign backing each of them drew, had emptied the agreement long before the date it fixed. Portugal held to that date all the same, which is why the agreement matters even though it failed. It is in the atlas as the point where decolonisation in Angola turns into a war between Angolans.

## angola-independence-1975

`event`

**Before**

> Portugal withdrew from Angola on 11 November 1975 without transferring power to any of the three liberation movements, which were already at war with one another.

**After**

> Portuguese sovereignty over Angola lapsed at midnight on 11 November 1975 without being transferred to anyone: the Alvor agreement had collapsed, and the High Commissioner left Luanda having handed power, in the formula used at the time, to the Angolan people. The MPLA, which held the capital, proclaimed the People's Republic of Angola; the FNLA and UNITA proclaimed a rival republic at Huambo. Cuban troops and a South African column were already in the country, and the civil war that followed ran, with interruptions, until 2002. Portugal recognised the MPLA government in February 1976. In the atlas it is the end of the Angolan branch of the colonial war and the clearest case of a decolonisation that handed power to nobody.

## angola-war-begins-1961

`event`

**Before**

> Attacks on prisons in Luanda on 4 February 1961 and the uprisings in the north from 15 March opened the armed conflict in Angola, the first front of the colonial war.

**After**

> An attack on the prisons and police stations of Luanda on 4 February 1961, meant to free political prisoners, was beaten off with heavy losses among the attackers and followed by reprisals in the African quarters of the city. On 15 March a far larger rising in the coffee districts of the north, organised by the UPA, killed several hundred settlers and a much greater number of African workers, and was answered by a Portuguese campaign that retook the region over the following months. The MPLA claims 4 February as the start of its armed struggle and the claim has been contested since; what is not contested is that the two risings together opened thirteen years of war. Salazar's answer was to reinforce Angola at speed rather than negotiate, a choice that committed the Estado Novo to fighting on three fronts. It is in the atlas as the first front of the colonial war and the beginning of what eventually consumed the regime.

## azores-agreement-1943

`event`

**Before**

> Invoking the alliance of 1373, Britain obtained the use of bases in the Azores from Portugal in August 1943, while Portugal remained formally neutral.

**After**

> Britain invoked the alliance of 1373 to ask for facilities in the Azores, and the agreement signed on 17 August 1943 gave British forces the use of the islands from October, with the airfield at Lajes on Terceira the most important of them. Portugal remained formally neutral, and Salazar presented the concession as an obligation of the oldest alliance rather than a choice between belligerents. American forces obtained their own access the following year after separate negotiation. The facilities mattered to the Battle of the Atlantic, and the regime spent the credit they earned for a decade afterwards. It is in the atlas because it is where Portuguese neutrality is converted into a standing claim on the Western alliance, one the regime drew on again at the founding of NATO in 1949.

## battle-of-the-lys-1918

`event`

**Before**

> On 9 April 1918 the German spring offensive broke the sector held by the Portuguese Expeditionary Corps on the Lys, the heaviest single loss suffered by Portuguese forces in the war.

**After**

> The German offensive of 9 April 1918 fell on the Lys sector held by the 2nd Division of the Portuguese Expeditionary Corps, which was below strength, long overdue for relief and due to be withdrawn within days. The division was broken in a morning; Portuguese losses that day are usually given as some seven thousand men killed, wounded or taken prisoner, most of them prisoners. Accounts differ over how much of the collapse belongs to the state of the division and how much to the weight of the attack, but not over the political effect. The defeat became the central grievance of the officers who had been sent to Flanders against the war party in Lisbon. It is in the atlas as the point where the First Republic's war policy turns into the army's quarrel with the republic itself.

## cabral-assassinated-1973

`event`

**Before**

> Cabral was shot dead outside his house in Conakry on 20 January 1973 by members of his own movement. Portuguese involvement through the PIDE has been alleged since the day it happened and is argued over; the plotters were caught and executed by the PAIGC.

**After**

> Amílcar Cabral was shot dead outside his house in Conakry on the night of 20 January 1973 by members of the PAIGC, the movement he had founded and led. The killing came with an attempt to seize the movement's leadership; the plotters were caught within days by Guinean and PAIGC forces and executed after trial. Whether the Portuguese political police organised the plot, encouraged it, or merely knew of it has been argued since the day it happened, and the surviving Portuguese record does not settle it. The PAIGC held together under Aristides Pereira, declared independence in September 1973 and was recognised by most of the world within a year, so the assassination did not have the effect intended. It is in the atlas both as the end of the most effective of the liberation leaders and as a link whose causes historians dispute.

## caetano-succeeds-salazar-1968

`event`

**Before**

> After Salazar was incapacitated in August 1968, Marcelo Caetano was appointed President of the Council on 27 September and promised a controlled liberalisation.

**After**

> Salazar was incapacitated in early August 1968 by a haemorrhage after a fall, and on 27 September President Américo Tomás appointed Marcelo Caetano President of the Council in his place. Caetano offered "evolution in continuity": some liberalisation of censorship and of the political police, a wider electoral roll, and reformist deputies allowed into the National Assembly, but no negotiated end to the colonial war and no opening to opposition parties. Salazar recovered enough to believe himself still in office and was never told otherwise; he died in July 1970. The limits of the opening were clear by 1970, and the officers who made the 1974 coup came out of the war Caetano had not ended. It is in the atlas as the hinge between the regime built by one man and the six years in which his successor tried to reform it without touching what made reform necessary.

## carnation-revolution-1974

`event`

**Before**

> On 25 April 1974 the Armed Forces Movement overthrew the Estado Novo in a nearly bloodless coup that became a revolution.

**After**

> Units led by the Armed Forces Movement — a clandestine organisation of middle-ranking officers formed over the previous year around the colonial war and a grievance about promotions — left barracks in the early hours of 25 April 1974 on a plan drawn up by Otelo Saraiva de Carvalho, and held Lisbon by the morning. Marcelo Caetano surrendered at the Carmo barracks that afternoon and insisted on handing power to General António de Spínola, whom the movement had not chosen but accepted as a figurehead. Crowds ignored the broadcast instruction to stay indoors, and the carnations put into gun barrels gave the day its name; the only deaths were of civilians shot by the political police outside its Lisbon headquarters. The coup itself was over within a day, but it opened nineteen months in which the form of the new regime, the ownership of the economy and the future of the African territories were all undecided. It is the pivot of this part of the atlas: most of what follows descends from it.

## constituent-assembly-election-1975

`event`

**Before**

> The election of 25 April 1975, the first with universal suffrage, gave the Socialist and Popular Democratic parties a clear majority over the Communists and their allies.

**After**

> The election of 25 April 1975, held on the first anniversary of the coup, was the first in Portugal with universal adult suffrage and the first free election since the 1920s; turnout was over ninety per cent. It chose the assembly that wrote the 1976 constitution, and the parties had signed a pact with the Armed Forces Movement beforehand that fixed the military's place in the new institutions whatever the result. The Socialists took about thirty-eight per cent and the Popular Democrats about twenty-six, against about twelve and a half for the Communists, a result far from the direction the provisional government was then taking. The distance between the vote and the government's course is the substance of the "hot summer" that followed it. It is in the atlas as the first measurement of what the country actually wanted, and as evidence in every later argument about 1975.

## constitution-1911

`event`

**Before**

> The Constituent Assembly approved a parliamentary constitution with a weak presidency, the framework of the First Republic until 1926.

**After**

> The Constituent Assembly elected in May 1911 approved the republic's first constitution on 21 August: a two-chamber congress, a president elected by that congress rather than by the country and unable to dissolve it, and the separation of church and state already legislated in April. It was the framework of the First Republic until the coup of 28 May 1926, a period that produced more than forty governments. Its critics, then and since, argue that a president who could not break a deadlock left revolt as the only way out of one; others answer that the parties, not the text, were what failed. It is in the atlas as the constitutional form of the regime whose instability is the subject of most of the events around it.

## constitution-1933

`event`

**Before**

> Approved by plebiscite on 19 March 1933, the constitution founded the Estado Novo: a corporatist, single-party authoritarian state.

**After**

> The constitution drafted under Salazar's direction was approved by plebiscite on 19 March 1933 and gave the Estado Novo its legal shape: a corporative state, a National Assembly in the hands of a single party, a head of government answerable to the President of the Republic rather than to parliament, and a corporative chamber for organised interests. Abstentions were counted as votes in favour. Censorship, the political police and the ban on other parties were built alongside the text rather than written into it. Historians differ over how far the result was fascist and how far it was conservative and Catholic authoritarianism, and over how much the corporative machinery ever governed anything. It is in the atlas as the founding document of the regime that fell on 25 April 1974.

## constitution-1976

`event`

**Before**

> The Constituent Assembly approved the democratic constitution on 2 April 1976, keeping the Council of the Revolution as a military guardian until the revision of 1982.

**After**

> The constitution approved on 2 April 1976 and in force from 25 April was written by the assembly elected a year earlier, within the limits the parties had accepted in their pacts with the Armed Forces Movement. It established a parliamentary republic with a directly elected president, kept a Council of the Revolution of officers as guardian of the constitution, and committed the state to a transition to socialism and to the irreversibility of the 1975 nationalisations. The revision of 1982 abolished the Council and moved the guardianship to a constitutional court; that of 1989 opened the nationalised sector to sale. It is in the atlas as the settlement of the revolutionary period — a founding document written, unusually, in the knowledge that parts of it would have to be revised.

## coup-28-may-1926

`event`

**Before**

> The military uprising that began in Braga under Gomes da Costa on 28 May 1926 ended the First Republic and opened the Military Dictatorship.

**After**

> The rising began in Braga on 28 May 1926 under General Gomes da Costa, met almost no resistance as it moved south, and brought down the government of António Maria da Silva within two days. Its authors agreed on ending the parliamentary republic and on very little else: Gomes da Costa was pushed aside by his own side within six weeks and Óscar Carmona emerged as president. The dictatorship that followed had no constitution and no answer to the deficit, which is the opening through which Salazar entered the government in 1928. In the atlas it is the end of the First Republic and the first link in the chain that produces the Estado Novo.

## coup-attempt-11-march-1975

`event`

**Before**

> An attempt by officers associated with Spínola, including an air attack on the RAL-1 barracks in Lisbon, collapsed the same day. Spínola left the country, and the balance inside the Armed Forces Movement shifted sharply to the left.

**After**

> On 11 March 1975 officers associated with António de Spínola moved against the left of the Armed Forces Movement, with an air attack on the RAL-1 artillery barracks in Lisbon at its centre; the attempt found little support and collapsed the same day. Spínola left the country that evening, first for Spain and then for Brazil. The reaction was immediate and went far beyond the plotters: the Council of the Revolution was created within days, the banks and insurance companies were nationalised, and the officers who had wanted a slower course lost the argument. Whether the day was a coup attempt, a provocation, or a badly judged reaction to a rumour of a right-wing plot is still argued; the shift in the balance of power that followed is not. It is in the atlas because the largest economic changes of 1975 descend from a failed operation rather than from a decision taken on its merits.

## delgado-candidacy-1958

`event`

**Before**

> General Humberto Delgado ran against the regime's candidate in the presidential election of 8 June 1958; the campaign drew large crowds and the official result was widely disbelieved.

**After**

> General Humberto Delgado stood against the regime's candidate Américo Tomás in the presidential election of 8 June 1958, and answered a journalist's question about what he would do with Salazar with the phrase that made his campaign: obviously, I will dismiss him. The campaign drew crowds of a size the Estado Novo had not seen against it, and the official result of about a quarter of the vote was widely disbelieved. The regime's answer was to end direct election of the president altogether, by constitutional revision the following year. Delgado was dismissed from the air force, went into exile, and was murdered by the political police at the Spanish border in 1965. It is in the atlas as the moment the opposition's strength became measurable, and as the reason the one opening the regime had left was closed.

## eanes-elected-1976

`event`

**Before**

> Eanes, who had commanded the operation of 25 November 1975, was elected president in June 1976 in the first round, with the support of the Socialists, the Social Democrats and the CDS.

**After**

> Ramalho Eanes, the officer who had commanded the operation of 25 November 1975, was elected President of the Republic on 27 June 1976 in the first round, with about sixty per cent of the vote and the support of the Socialists, the Social Democrats and the CDS. The office he entered was the one the 1976 constitution had just created: directly elected, with the Council of the Revolution beside it. He served two terms, to 1986, and presided over the return of the military to barracks and the end of the Council in the 1982 revision. It is in the atlas as the completion of the institutional settlement begun by the constitution, and as the point where the officers of 1974 begin to leave politics.

## east-timor-independence-2002

`event`

**Before**

> East Timor became independent on 20 May 2002 after the 1999 referendum and a period of United Nations administration.

**After**

> East Timor became independent on 20 May 2002, twenty-seven years after Portugal's administration ended and Indonesia invaded. The path to it ran through the referendum of 30 August 1999, in which about seventy-eight per cent voted against autonomy within Indonesia, the violence by militias that followed the result, an Australian-led international force, and two and a half years of United Nations transitional administration. Portugal had never recognised the annexation and had kept the question at the United Nations throughout, which is why the case remained open long after it had ceased to be news. In the atlas it is the last of the territories to leave, and the one whose decolonisation took a quarter of a century longer than the rest.

## eec-accession-1986

`event`

**Before**

> Portugal became a member of the European Economic Community on 1 January 1986; the treaty had been signed at the Jerónimos monastery on 12 June 1985.

**After**

> Portugal became a member of the European Economic Community on 1 January 1986, alongside Spain, after nine years of negotiation; the treaty had been signed at the Jerónimos monastery in Lisbon on 12 June 1985. Membership had been an aim of every government since 1977 and was defended as much for what it settled politically as for what it offered economically: a democracy inside the Community was harder to overturn. Structural funds, the opening of the economy and a decade of convergence followed, and so did the terms on which Portugal later entered monetary union. It is in the atlas as the point where the country's external orientation shifts from the Atlantic and the former empire to Europe.

## eec-application-1977

`event`

**Before**

> The Soares government submitted Portugal's application for membership of the European Economic Community on 28 March 1977.

**After**

> The first constitutional government, led by Mário Soares, submitted Portugal's application for membership of the European Economic Community on 28 March 1977, eleven months after the constitution came into force. The application was as much political as economic: accession was a way of anchoring a democracy that had almost not survived 1975, and of choosing Europe over the alternatives the revolutionary period had put on the table. Negotiations opened in 1978 and took until 1985, held up by the Community's own agricultural and budgetary disputes as much as by Portugal. It is in the atlas as the first step of the process that ends with accession in 1986.

## euro-adoption-1999

`event`

**Before**

> Portugal was among the eleven states that entered the third stage of monetary union on 1 January 1999, fixing the escudo against the euro; notes and coins followed in 2002. The interest rates that came with it are central to every later argument about the Portuguese slump.

**After**

> Portugal was among the eleven states that entered the third stage of economic and monetary union on 1 January 1999, fixing the escudo irrevocably against the euro; notes and coins replaced escudos in 2002. Qualifying had required several years of deficit and inflation reduction, and admission was read at the time as confirmation that the country had converged with the European core. The fall in interest rates that came with it produced a decade of private borrowing and a current account deficit, and every later account of the Portuguese slump argues about how much of that was the euro and how much was domestic policy. It is in the atlas because the arguments about 2011 begin here.

## germany-declares-war-1916

`event`

**Before**

> After Portugal requisitioned German merchant ships in the Tagus at British request in February 1916, Germany declared war on 9 March, bringing Portugal formally into the First World War.

**After**

> Portugal requisitioned the German and Austrian merchant ships interned in the Tagus and other ports on 23 February 1916, at Britain's request and under the terms of the alliance; Germany declared war on 9 March. Portugal was already fighting German colonial forces in Angola and Mozambique, but the declaration brought it formally into the European war and made possible the expeditionary corps sent to Flanders the following year. The Democrats around Afonso Costa had pressed for belligerence, partly to secure the colonies and the country's standing at the peace, and the war they obtained divided the republic more deeply than anything else it did. It is in the atlas as the decision from which the Lys, the Sidónio Pais coup and much of the army's later politics follow.

## goa-annexed-1961

`event`

**Before**

> Indian forces took Goa, Daman and Diu on 18–19 December 1961, ending four and a half centuries of Portuguese rule in India.

**After**

> Indian forces entered Goa, Daman and Diu on 18 December 1961 and the Portuguese governor-general surrendered the following day, ending four and a half centuries of Portuguese rule in India. Salazar had ordered a defence to the last man and refused to negotiate; the garrison was small, isolated and could not be reinforced. India presented the action as the completion of its own decolonisation and much of the world accepted that; Portugal called it an invasion, kept the territories on its books until 1974, and drew from the episode the lesson that its position in Africa would have to be held by force. In the atlas it stands between the outbreak of the war in Angola and the wars that followed, and it is where the regime's isolation at the United Nations becomes plain.

## guinea-war-begins-1963

`event`

**Before**

> The PAIGC attack on the barracks at Tite in January 1963 opened the war in Portuguese Guinea, the front the Portuguese army came closest to losing.

**After**

> The PAIGC attacked the barracks at Tite, south of Bissau, on 23 January 1963, opening the armed struggle in Portuguese Guinea. Guinea was the smallest of the three fronts and the one the Portuguese army came closest to losing: by the late 1960s the PAIGC held or contested most of the countryside, ran administration in the areas it held, and had support across the borders in Senegal and Guinea-Conakry. It was in Guinea that António de Spínola commanded, and the conclusions he drew there became Portugal e o Futuro in 1974. It is in the atlas as the front whose course did most to convince Portuguese officers that the war could not be won.

## law-of-separation-1911

`event`

**Before**

> The decree of 20 April 1911, drafted by Afonso Costa, separated Church and State, secularised civil life and placed Church property under state control.

**After**

> The decree of 20 April 1911, drafted by Afonso Costa, separated church and state: it ended the Catholic religion's official standing, transferred church property to the state, subjected worship to registration and public control, expelled or restricted the religious orders, and secularised registers, cemeteries, marriage and education. It was the most consequential act of the republic's first year and the most divisive, provoking the resistance of much of the clergy and of the rural north, papal condemnation, and a conflict that was only settled by the concordat of 1940. Historians differ on whether the law's anticlerical severity was the republic's founding mistake or an unavoidable consequence of what the monarchy had been. It is in the atlas as the origin of a division that outlasted the regime that created it.

## legislative-election-1976

`event`

**Before**

> The first election to the Assembly of the Republic under the new constitution was held on 25 April 1976, a year to the day after the constituent election. The Socialists came first without a majority and formed a minority government.

**After**

> The first election to the Assembly of the Republic under the new constitution was held on 25 April 1976, two years to the day after the coup and a year after the constituent election. The Socialists came first with about thirty-five per cent, short of a majority, and Mário Soares formed a minority government; the Popular Democrats, the CDS and the Communists took the rest, in roughly the proportions of the year before. It confirmed the pattern of the constituent election and made clear that no single party would govern the new republic alone. It is in the atlas as the ordinary politics that the revolutionary period ends in.

## macau-handover-1999

`event`

**Before**

> Portugal transferred the administration of Macau to China on 20 December 1999 under the Joint Declaration of 1987.

**After**

> Portugal transferred the administration of Macau to the People's Republic of China at midnight on 20 December 1999, under the joint declaration signed in 1987, which provided for a special administrative region with its own system for fifty years. Sovereignty had been an open question for a quarter of a century: after 1974 Portugal offered to withdraw, China preferred to wait, and from 1979 Macau was described as Chinese territory under Portuguese administration. The transfer was negotiated, unhurried and without incident, which is what makes it unlike every other ending in this part of the atlas. It is the last territory to leave Portuguese administration, and the point at which the empire is finished as an administrative fact.

## mozambique-war-begins-1964

`event`

**Before**

> FRELIMO opened the armed struggle in Mozambique with an attack at Chai, in Cabo Delgado, on 25 September 1964.

**After**

> FRELIMO opened the armed struggle in Mozambique with an attack on the administrative post at Chai, in Cabo Delgado, on 25 September 1964. The war spread through the northern districts and, after Eduardo Mondlane's assassination in 1969 and Samora Machel's rise, into Tete around the Cahora Bassa dam. It was the third and last front of the colonial war, and by 1974 it tied down the largest of the three Portuguese forces without either side being able to finish it. It is in the atlas as the front that made the war continental in scale, and one of the three chains that end at 25 April 1974.

## nationalisations-1975

`event`

**Before**

> In the days after the failed coup of 11 March 1975, the Council of the Revolution nationalised the banks and insurance companies, the core of the revolutionary economic programme.

**After**

> Three days after the failed coup of 11 March 1975, the newly created Council of the Revolution nationalised the Portuguese banks, and the insurance companies followed the next day. Because the banks held much of the country's industry, the decision carried the largest economic groups with it, and the nationalisations of electricity, steel, cement, transport and other sectors over the following months completed a state sector that had not existed in April 1974. The agrarian reform in the Alentejo ran in parallel and by a different route, through occupations later given legal form. The 1976 constitution declared the nationalisations irreversible; the revision of 1989 removed that clause and privatisation followed through the 1990s. It is in the atlas as the moment the revolution changed who owned the economy, and as the direct consequence of a coup that failed.

## nato-founding-1949

`event`

**Before**

> Portugal was one of the twelve founding signatories of the North Atlantic Treaty in Washington on 4 April 1949, the only non-democratic member at its foundation.

**After**

> Portugal was one of the twelve states that signed the North Atlantic Treaty in Washington on 4 April 1949, and the only signatory that was not a democracy. Salazar had hesitated — the alliance meant commitments and foreign inspection of Portuguese forces — and accepted on the argument that the Azores made Portugal necessary to the Atlantic, which the 1943 agreement and the war had already demonstrated. Membership modernised the officer corps, sent officers abroad to train, and gave the regime a standing among the Western powers that survived the early years of the colonial war. Historians disagree over how much protection it actually bought at the United Nations after 1961. It is in the atlas because the army that made the 1974 coup was in part an army NATO had trained.

## noite-sangrenta-1921

`event`

**Before**

> During the night of 19 October 1921 a military uprising in Lisbon ended with the murder of the former prime minister António Granjo, Machado Santos and other republican figures.

**After**

> A military rising in Lisbon on 19 October 1921 overthrew the government of António Granjo, and during the night that followed groups of armed men took Granjo, the former president Machado Santos and other republican figures from custody and murdered them. The killings were done in the name of a rising that had no plan for what came next, and no one was ever effectively punished for them. Historians treat the night as the point at which the First Republic lost the last of its credit with the officers and the middle class alike, and it is regularly cited among the causes of the acceptance that greeted the coup of 1926. It is in the atlas as the clearest single measure of how far the republic's politics had decayed.

## portugal-backs-franco-1936

`event`

**Before**

> From the outbreak of the Spanish war in July 1936 Salazar's government gave the Nationalist side territory to move through, supplies, a broadcasting platform and volunteers, and returned republican refugees. Historians differ on the weight of this help to the outcome; they agree it was substantial and that a republican victory in Spain was read in Lisbon as a threat to the regime's survival.

**After**

> From the outbreak of the Spanish war in July 1936, Salazar's government supported the Nationalist side: it allowed men and supplies to cross Portuguese territory, gave the rebels a diplomatic and broadcasting platform, returned Spanish republican refugees who crossed the border, and let some eight thousand volunteers — the Viriatos — go and fight. Portugal joined the Non-Intervention Committee at the end of the year and continued to help in practice. Historians differ over how much the help weighed in the outcome; they agree that it was substantial and that Lisbon read a republican victory in Spain as a threat to the Estado Novo's own survival. The relationship was formalised in the Iberian Pact of 1939, which kept both regimes out of the world war and each out of the other's way. It is in the atlas as the point where the regime's foreign policy is set for the next twenty years.

## portugal-e-o-futuro-1974

`event`

**Before**

> General António de Spínola's book, published in February 1974, argued publicly that the colonial war could not be won by military means.

**After**

> General António de Spínola's Portugal e o Futuro was published on 22 February 1974 and argued that the colonial war could not be won by military means and that the answer was a federal political solution. Coming from the man who had commanded in Guinea and was then deputy chief of the general staff, and passing the censorship, the book made public what officers had been saying privately. Caetano later wrote that reading it convinced him a coup was coming; Spínola and the chief of staff Costa Gomes were dismissed in March after refusing to appear at a ceremony of loyalty to the government. The Armed Forces Movement, which had formed for reasons of its own, acquired in Spínola a figure the public knew. It is in the atlas as the point where the officers' argument becomes a public one, two months before the coup.

## republic-proclaimed-1910

`event`

**Before**

> The 5 October revolution in Lisbon ended the constitutional monarchy; Manuel II left for exile and a provisional government led by Teófilo Braga took power.

**After**

> A rising by republican civilians and units of the army and navy began in Lisbon on the night of 3 October 1910 and the Republic was proclaimed from the balcony of the city hall on the morning of the 5th, after the warships in the Tagus had shelled the royal palace. Manuel II left the country for exile in England, and a provisional government under Teófilo Braga took power. The revolution was made in Lisbon and largely accepted elsewhere, which is one reason the republic then struggled to govern a country that had not made it. It is in the atlas as the beginning of the sequence — republic, dictatorship, Estado Novo, revolution — that this part of the map follows.

## revolt-14-may-1915

`event`

**Before**

> A rising of armed civilians and naval units in Lisbon on 14 May 1915 overthrew the Pimenta de Castro government after several days of fighting with hundreds of casualties, and returned the Democratic Party to power.

**After**

> Armed civilians, naval units and part of the Lisbon garrison rose on 14 May 1915 against the government of General Pimenta de Castro, which had been appointed by President Manuel de Arriaga and had governed without parliament while readmitting monarchists to public life. Several days of fighting in the capital left hundreds dead and wounded before the government surrendered; Pimenta de Castro and Arriaga were removed and the Democratic Party returned to power. The revolt settled the immediate question in favour of the party, and settled nothing about the method: force had again decided a political dispute, and it did so again in 1917, 1919, 1921 and 1926. It is in the atlas as an early link in the chain of interventions that ends with the First Republic's fall.

## salazar-finance-minister-1928

`event`

**Before**

> António de Oliveira Salazar accepted the Finance portfolio on 27 April 1928 on condition of a veto over the spending of every other ministry.

**After**

> António de Oliveira Salazar, a professor of finance at Coimbra, accepted the finance portfolio in the military dictatorship's government on 27 April 1928, having refused it briefly in 1926, and on the condition that no ministry could spend without his agreement. The condition was the point: it made him, rather than the government, responsible for the deficit that the dictatorship had no other answer to. Balanced budgets and a stabilised currency followed within two years, and were presented as proof that the new order worked where the republic had not. From that position he took the presidency of the council in 1932 and wrote the constitution of 1933. It is in the atlas as the beginning of forty years of one man's power, entered through a technical appointment.

## salazar-president-of-council-1932

`event`

**Before**

> On 5 July 1932 Salazar was appointed head of government, a position he held until 1968.

**After**

> Salazar was appointed President of the Council of Ministers on 5 July 1932 by President Óscar Carmona, and held the office until September 1968. The appointment made the civilian who had balanced the budget the head of a government the military had run since 1926, and the constitution he had drafted was put to plebiscite eight months later. In the atlas it is the point at which the Military Dictatorship becomes the Estado Novo in fact, before it does so in law.

## sidonio-pais-assassinated-1918

`event`

**Before**

> Sidónio Pais was shot at Rossio station in Lisbon on 14 December 1918, ending the República Nova after a year and opening a period of acute instability.

**After**

> Sidónio Pais was shot at the Rossio station in Lisbon on the evening of 14 December 1918 by José Júlio da Costa, and died the same night. His "New Republic" had lasted a year and depended almost entirely on him: within weeks of his death the monarchist rising in the north and a counter-rising in Lisbon had begun, and the party system he had displaced returned without recovering its authority. The killing was condemned by every side and the funeral was enormous, which is part of why later authoritarian movements claimed him. It is in the atlas as the end of the republic's one experiment with a presidential regime, and as the opening of its most unstable years.

## sidonio-pais-coup-1917

`event`

**Before**

> Between 5 and 8 December 1917 Sidónio Pais overthrew the Democratic government of Afonso Costa and founded the presidentialist "República Nova".

**After**

> Sidónio Pais, an officer and former minister who had been Portugal's envoy in Berlin, rose against the Democratic government on 5 December 1917 and took Lisbon after three days of fighting; Afonso Costa was arrested and President Bernardino Machado deposed and exiled. Pais governed as head of a junta and then, from April 1918, as a president elected by direct universal male suffrage — a form the 1911 constitution did not provide for — with parliament reduced and the parties pushed aside. He restored relations with the Church, courted Catholic and monarchist support, and kept Portugal in the war while withdrawing support from the expeditionary corps. The regime, called the New Republic, ended with his assassination a year later. It is in the atlas as the first successful military intervention of the republic and a precedent for those that followed.

## spinola-resigns-1974

`event`

**Before**

> After the confrontation of 28 September 1974, when a demonstration of the "silent majority" he had called for was blocked by barricades, Spínola resigned as president of the Republic at the end of the month and was replaced by Costa Gomes.

**After**

> António de Spínola called on the "silent majority" to demonstrate in Lisbon on 28 September 1974 in support of his own authority against the Armed Forces Movement; barricades put up by left-wing parties and by military units loyal to the movement stopped the demonstration from happening. Two days later he resigned the presidency and was replaced by Costa Gomes. The confrontation settled the argument the two sides had had since April over the pace of decolonisation and over who governed, and it settled it against the president. Spínola's next attempt, in March 1975, ended in exile. It is in the atlas as the point where the revolution's direction stops being decided by the figure the coup had put at its head.

## troika-bailout-2011

`event`

**Before**

> Portugal requested financial assistance on 6 April 2011 and signed the memorandum with the European Commission, the ECB and the IMF on 17 May, opening three years of adjustment.

**After**

> Portugal requested external financial assistance on 6 April 2011, after the parliament had rejected the fourth austerity package and the government of José Sócrates had resigned, and with borrowing costs at a level the treasury could not sustain. The memorandum with the European Commission, the European Central Bank and the International Monetary Fund was signed on 17 May and provided about seventy-eight billion euros over three years against a programme of consolidation, tax rises and structural change. The adjustment ran to mid-2014 and coincided with a deep recession, high unemployment and a wave of emigration; how much of that was the programme and how much the crisis it answered is the argument the record's sources are about. ## un-admission-1955

`event`

**Before**

> Portugal entered the United Nations on 14 December 1955 as part of a package of sixteen admissions negotiated between the blocs.

**After**

> Portugal joined the United Nations on 14 December 1955, in a package of sixteen admissions agreed between the Western and Soviet blocs to unblock a membership question that had been frozen since 1946. Membership had been vetoed until then, and the regime had treated the exclusion as evidence of hostility rather than as a problem to solve. Once inside, Portugal faced the opposite difficulty: from the late 1950s the General Assembly's anti-colonial majority made the African territories a standing item, and Lisbon answered by renaming them overseas provinces and denying that Chapter XI applied. It is in the atlas as the beginning of the diplomatic pressure that runs through the whole colonial war.
