What this is

A map and a timeline over the same thing: a graph. Events are nodes. The links between them are edges, each one carrying a type, a confidence level and a written argument for why the link is there. You pick an event, follow its consequences forward, and — this is the part the format exists for — see which other branches fed the endpoint you arrived at, so that the path you happened to walk does not look like the explanation.

Time is a window, not a moment. The shaded band on the timeline is the window; drag either end to widen or narrow it, drag the middle to slide it, or double-click a year to snap it to that decade. The arrow keys move whichever end has the focus. The map shows the events whose own interval overlaps the window — an event that ran for ten years is in every window it touches — and the territories of the window's last year. The / key jumps to the search box, which finds events by title and actors by any of their names, and places by any of theirs.

An event happens at a place, and a place is a record of its own rather than a pair of coordinates typed onto each event: thirty-seven of the records in the current test dataset happened in Lisbon, and Lisbon is written once. Clicking a place name — on an event, or at the head of a stack of marks on the map — opens what happened there, in order, with the events outside the window faded rather than hidden, and the actors who turn up there most.

Actors also stand in relations to one another, which is the one thing an edge cannot say: an edge runs between events. A relation is dated and typed — a regime of a state, a body part of another, a person or a state that is a member of one, a state succeeded by another, two actors allied — and it carries its sources like any other argument. Relations have no page of their own: they are listed on the card of the actor at either end, so Portugal's card names its four regimes and the Estado Novo's card says which state it was a regime of and what belonged to it.

Who held a post is not a relation but an office and the tenures at it, because one person may hold one post more than once. An office has a card of its own and is found in the search box: it names the actor the post belongs to, what kind of post it is, and every turn at it in order, each row opening the person who held it. On the card of an actor that owns posts, each office is also a strip of its holders over that actor's own years, so a century of prime ministers reads as a row of bars; clicking one opens the person. An office asserts that the post existed and nothing else, so it cites nothing and the card says so; the tenures carry the sources.

A narrative is the other way of reading the same graph: one person's account of it, walked step by step. Each step points at an event or at a link and carries the narrator's own paragraph on why that step follows; the map, the graph and the timeline follow the step, and the whole walk is marked so you can see where it is going. A narrative changes nothing it walks — it is prose beside the graph, signed and cited like everything else — and where two accounts of the same period disagree, both stand and you compare them. The reader is the list of narratives, in the header beside the bibliography, where they are arranged by the centuries they cross so that two accounts of the same years sit side by side; every event also says which narratives pass through it.

The first slice is Portuguese expansion, 1415 to 1580, chosen because its consequences cross continents, which is what the format is for. If the model survives that period it generalises; if it does not, better to find out on a small map.

The hard part was never the map. No dataset states that one thing caused another across four centuries and two oceans: that has to be written, by a person, with sources. So this is editorial work with a map attached rather than a data visualisation, and every claim in it is signed and cited. Nothing here is generated by a language model — not the summaries, not the arguments, not the disputes. A model will produce a fluent causal story about any pair of events, including unrelated ones, and in a history site that is not a small flaw but total failure.

Every claim rests on a cited source, and a source is a record like any other: written once, cited by reference, and readable from either end. Clicking the title in any citation opens that work and everything in the atlas that rests on it — with the page each record names, and with a work cited in dissent from a link kept apart from the works cited for it. The whole list is the bibliography, generated from the records rather than maintained by hand.

How to read it

How to read a card

Clicking a mark on the map, a bar on the timeline or a node in the graph opens a card in the panel on the right. Every card is built the same way, whether it is about an event, an actor or a place.

At the top is the head: the title, one line saying when and where it happened and which lane it is drawn in, and — on an event — the actors as small chips. Hovering a chip, or reaching it with the keyboard, shows what that actor did in this event; clicking it opens the actor. Under the head are the quiet ways out: the full entry, somebody else’s account on Wikipedia, and the way to disagree with the record.

Then the summary — the atlas’s own paragraph, and nothing else.

Everything else is behind a header you can open, with a count beside it: Consequences, Causes, Other branches, Sources, Part of, and on an actor Relations and Territory. Nothing has been taken away — the count tells you what is behind each header before you open it, and one click gets you there. Only one is open at a time, and the atlas remembers which one you last chose. A header on a section that holds a disputed link says so in the count (“Consequences (3, 1 disputed)”), so a closed section never hides an argument.

Which one opens by itself follows what you just did: walking a chain opens Consequences, arriving from a source’s card opens Sources, and otherwise it is whichever you had open last.

While you are following a chain, the path you have walked is a breadcrumb above the card. Each earlier step is a link back to itself, and going back to the middle of a path drops the steps after it: the path is the argument you are following, and the steps beyond the one you are re-reading were not taken.

Opening a record — an event, an actor, a place, a source, a narrative — adds an entry to your browser’s history, so Back returns to what you were reading. Moving the view does not: panning, zooming, dragging the time band or changing the lanes replaces the entry rather than adding one, or Back would walk you frame by frame through a drag. The card names its own Back link, so you can see where it goes before you press it.

The five edge types

An edge is one of caused, enabled, reacted-to, precondition-of or inspired. There is no generic type and there never will be: collapsing these into plain causation is how a map of history turns into cheap determinism. The type is doing real work, and choosing the right one of the five is part of what a reviewer checks.

The three confidence levels

Confidence is defined by evidence, so that it can be checked rather than felt.

consensus
The link is accepted. It requires at least two cited sources by different authors — a necessary condition, not a sufficient one: a reviewer still judges whether they really agree.
probable
Supported by the sources cited, with no known dissent. Most edges live here, and that is honest: it says the argument rests on the sources given and nobody has been found arguing the other way.
disputed
Qualified historians disagree about the link itself — whether it exists, what type it is, or how much it carries.

What did this lead to by a given year?

The consequences list on an event is one step out: what this directly led to. Under it, what did this lead to by a year is the other question — the whole downstream, however far away, cut at a year you choose. The list is ordered by how many steps away each event is and then by when it happened, and it says the type and confidence of the first step and marks a path that runs through a disputed link, so a long chain of weak links is never mistaken for a short chain of strong ones.

The year starts at the end of the window and can be set to anything. Choosing one of the answers walks the shortest path to it: it becomes the path in red, and you can then ask what else fed the event you arrived at — which is the convergence query again, from the other direction. While a year of your own is set, everything reachable is lit on the map, the graph and the timeline, fading with distance.

A disputed edge

A disputed edge stays in the atlas. Removing it would be picking a side, and being able to see the disagreement is more interesting than a clean answer. It is drawn as a dashed line on the map, marked in the panel, and it is never walked through silently: following a chain across one tells you that you have, and shows you who disagrees and what they cite. The dissenting citations are kept apart from the supporting ones, so a book that argues against a link is never displayed as evidence for it.

Records are never deleted. A record that turns out to be wrong or duplicated becomes a tombstone that still resolves the old link, pointing at whatever supersedes it: a URL someone shared keeps working.

An event inside another event

An event can be part of a larger one — a battle inside a war, a decree inside a revolution. The card of the smaller says what it is part of, the card of the larger lists its parts in order, and either link opens the other. This is a statement about what a thing was inside, never about what caused what: causation is an edge, with an argument and sources behind it, and being part of something is neither.

The timeline draws a parent as a thin rule along the top of its lane, spanning its parts. The graph draws it as a level of detail: zoomed out, an event's parts are drawn inside it as one mark carrying the count of what is folded in, and the links that ran to a part run to the parent instead; zooming in takes them apart again. Nothing you are holding is ever folded away — the open event, the path you have walked, a lens, a narrative's steps — and no mark moves when the rest fold, so the picture does not rearrange itself under you. Focus only on this on a parent's card narrows the whole atlas to that event and everything inside it.

A large event is the ground, not a dot

Some events are the wrong shape for a mark: a world war, a pandemic, a long regional crisis. Where a contributor has written how wide an event's reach was, or where its parts fall in more than one lane, the atlas draws it as ground rather than as a point — a pale band the height of the drawing under the timeline's bars, named on the axis, and on the map a wash over the lane it belongs to. An event that spans the whole map gets no wash at all, because a tint over everything would film over every coastline and territory; it is named in the map's corner instead. The bar and the mark stay where they were, so a large event can still be opened and still reached with the keyboard, and its card says which of the three it is and why.

Reach is a claim about the event and not a way of making it look important: nothing about it changes how big the mark is. That is still the number of links and actors the record actually holds.

Beside it, in the map's bottom-left corner, is the count of the events in this window that have no place at all and are therefore on the timeline only — the picture saying what it is not drawing, which is not the same as the picture being wrong.

The lens: one record's events, and nothing else

The card of an actor, a place or a source offers show only these. It puts a lens on the whole atlas: the map, the graph and the timeline then hold the events that record is involved in and nothing else, with the links between two of them still drawn. This is not the same as selecting: selecting emphasises and leaves everything else in view, and the lens removes. The header says which lens is on and is the way out of it, and the address bar carries it — ?focus=actor:salazar — so a lens can be sent in a link. The window of time, the path you have walked and the question about a year all keep working inside it. Search is not narrowed: a record the lens does not keep is still found, listed, and marked as outside.

The map and the timeline, looking at the same place

Pan or zoom the map and the timeline follows it: the lanes then hold only the events whose place is on screen, and a small line above them says how many of how many are in view. The event you have open and the path you have walked are never taken away by it — a chain that runs off the edge of the map is still a chain — and show the world puts every event back without moving the map. The visible area is in the address bar (?bbox=-10,36,-6,43), so a view of one coast can be sent in a link and opens on that coast. The graph view has no viewport of its own and is never narrowed this way; it says so while the map is holding one.

The wheel over the timeline narrows or widens the window of time around the year under the pointer, and dragging its empty ground slides the window; the lanes themselves never move, because they stay on the whole span of the data. A click on nothing — the sea, the empty ground of a lane — puts down whatever you were holding: the open event and the walked path are cleared. Dragging never does that, and a click on a territory still opens its actor. The edges between the map, the timeline and the panel can be dragged to resize them; a double-click on an edge puts it back. Those sizes are yours and stay in your browser: they are not in the link, because a link is what you were looking at and not how you had arranged your window.

What the lanes are

The timeline's lanes and the graph's bands are one thing — a grouping of events — and it is a choice, next to Map | Graph in the header. The default is no grouping: the timeline packs the bars into as many unlabelled rows as it takes for none of them to overlap, and the graph drops its bands. The other three are one lane per actor, per place and per region. For actors and places the lanes are the six with the most events in the window, plus Other, and the picker lets you choose your own and put them in your own order; the choice is in the link (?group=actor&lanes=salazar,paigc).

An event is drawn in exactly one lane, never repeated across them: an event with four actors is one event, and drawing it four times would turn a count of events into a count of participations without saying so. Which lane is decided mechanically — the heaviest of its actors among the lanes on screen, that is, the one with the most events in the window — and the event's card always says which lane it is in, by what rule, and what else it involves. A rule you cannot see is a rule you cannot check.

The graph view

The Graph button in the header replaces the map with a third drawing of the same records: every event a node, every link a line, so the whole web can be seen at once instead of one chain at a time. It is the same atlas — the same panel, the same search, the same window of time, the same link in the address bar — drawn another way.

Left to right is the year, on the same scale the timeline keeps, so the arrow of time is visible and nothing is ever placed by simulated physics. Down the page, one faint band per lane — whatever the grouping says, and no bands at all without one — and inside a band the events of a year are spread apart and ordered so that the lines between them come out short and cross each other as little as possible. The window of time is the shaded band across the picture: what falls outside it fades rather than leaving, so the web is always all there. Drag to move, scroll to zoom, double-click to go back; zoomed in, every node on screen is named.

Zoomed out, what is too close together to tell apart is drawn as one mark with a +3 beside it saying how many more are underneath, and the links between two such marks become one line, drawn heavier for how many it carries, in the commonest of their types and dashed if any single one of them is disputed. Nothing is dropped: the counts add the picture back up to every event. Zooming in splits the marks; clicking one lists what is under it in the panel and goes to the zoom where it comes apart. Events of different bands never merge, and neither does anything you are working with — the open event, the path you have walked, the branches drawn at it, a lens, a horizon, a narrative's steps and the events of an open actor always keep a mark of their own.

Clicking a node opens it in the panel. Clicking a node that the open one leads to walks the chain — the same step as the panel's follow button — and the path you have walked is drawn in red in the graph, on the map and on the timeline alike. When an event is open, the other branches that fed it, the ones you did not walk, are drawn filled in: that is the convergence query, as a picture.

The key to the lines

The five types are told apart by the pattern and weight of the line, not by colour: the atlas has one accent and it belongs to the path being followed. A disputed link is dashed whatever its type, as it is everywhere else in the atlas, and its type then reads in the weight of the line alone.

Territories, and where they stop

The territories layer draws who held which ground in one year: the last year of the window, which the band on the timeline says out loud. It covers 1886 to 2019 and nothing else: before 1886 the layer is simply empty, and that is not a gap to be filled in with guesswork; past 2019 it holds the last year the source covers and says so. The switch in the header turns it off. The coastlines have no switch: they are the ground everything else is read against, and a map without them is a scatter of dots.

The outlines come from CShapes 2.0 — Guy Schvitz, Luc Girardin, Seraina Rüegger, Nils B. Weidmann, Lars-Erik Cederman and Kristian Skrede Gleditsch, Mapping the International System, 1886-2019, Journal of Conflict Resolution 66(1), 2022, doi:10.1177/00220027211013563 — used under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, which is not the licence of the rest of the data. Coordinates have been simplified for size; every date, status, sovereign and capital is the dataset's own.

What the styles mean: a state holding its own ground is a solid wash with a thin outline in the same colour darkened; a dependency — colony, protectorate, mandate or occupied territory — is the lighter tint of whoever held it, outlined dotted, so an empire and its possessions read as one family and hovering names both; a dashed outline means the presence itself is disputed, the same as a dashed link.

What the colours mean, and what they do not

There are eight of them, and there is no legend. A hue here says one thing only: this is not the same territory as the one beside it. It does not say which polity, what kind of state, whose empire, or how large — two hundred polities share eight colours, so a legend would be a lie four times over. Who was where is answered by hovering a territory and by clicking it, which opens the actor's card and fills in everything it held.

Which actor gets which hue is not somebody's taste and is not stored on the records. It is computed from the borders themselves: tools/build-palette.mjs works out who touches whom in every period the outlines cover and colours that graph so that no two neighbours are ever alike, writing the result to data/geo/palette.json. The hue belongs to the actor, so a territory keeps its colour as the years pass — Angola is the same colour in 1975 as in 2019, and before 1975 it is Portugal's colour lightened, because that is what it was. Where eight is not enough — an empire that borders a quarter of the world cannot be told from every one of its neighbours — the file records who it had to share with rather than hiding it.

The colours sit under the two that matter more. The actor you have selected is filled in cobalt over every hue, and the path you have walked is drawn in madder over that. Territory colour is the ground; those two are the argument.

A border on this map is still a modern idea imposed on the past. It is drawn here only for a period that had such borders and a source that dates every change of them; earlier periods will need diffuse zones rather than outlines, which is what the presenceType field is for. The timeline's lanes are continents, derived from the coordinates of the place each event happened at rather than stored on the record, so that redrawing the lanes never means rewriting the records.

The full entry

What a card shows is a summary: a sentence or two, enough to know what the record is while you are following links through the graph. Under it, on every event, actor and place, is Read the full entry — a page of its own, at entry.html?id=…, with the record's dates and place, the actors in it, the relations it stands in, the narratives that walk through it, everything it rests on, and an extensive text about it.

That text is written by a person, from sources they cite, and it is not a redirect to somebody else's encyclopedia. Most records have no entry yet and their pages say so and ask for one: the atlas is a graph first, and the long form arrives record by record as people write it.

An entry is written in a deliberately small Markdown — paragraphs, two levels of heading, emphasis, lists, quotations, links to other records and to the web, and citation marks like [^some-source p. 12] that must name a work the record already cites. There is no raw HTML in it and there are no images: everything under data/ is somebody's contribution, and a page that let one of them write markup would be a page that let one of them write anything. The marks are numbered on the page in the order the entry names each work, and every one of them leads down to that work in the entry's own list of sources.

Disagreeing with a record, and taking a picture away

Every card ends with Discuss this record: it opens a correction issue against the repository, named for the record and carrying the address you were looking at, so whoever answers opens the same picture. A record here is an argument somebody made, and the way to disagree with one is in the open.

The map and the graph offer Export this view: the drawing on screen as an SVG file, with the stylesheet and the colours as your browser computed them written into it, so it opens anywhere. It is a picture and not the data — the data is the repository, in full, under the licences below.

On a phone

On a narrow screen the atlas stacks: the map or the graph takes the screen, the timeline is a strip under it, and the panel becomes a sheet that comes up from the bottom edge when you tap a mark and can be pushed back down to its handle when it is in the way — drag the handle, or tap it. The search takes a line of its own, and the grouping and the layer switches are behind Options. The map and the graph both pan under one finger.

Nothing about the address changes: a link opened on a phone and the same link opened on a desktop show the same records. How wide the panes are, which section of a card is open, and whether the sheet is up are yours and not part of the link.

The link to Wikipedia, and what it is not

An event, an actor or a place may carry the identifier of the Wikidata item for the same thing, and the titles of the Wikipedia articles about it. Where there is a title, the card offers Read more on Wikipedia — in your language when there is an article in it, otherwise in English, and it always says which — and the article's title also becomes one of the names the search box finds the record by.

That link is a way out of the atlas, not part of it. Everything the atlas asserts is in the record: the summary, the argument on each link, the confidence, the sources. Nothing here is written from an encyclopedia and nothing here is checked against one, and no article's text is in any record — only its title, which is a name. The identifier exists so that a record can be found again by a machine and so that a reader who wants somebody else's account can go and get it; it is never counted as evidence. An encyclopedia reports scholarship rather than being it, so a link that cites nothing but Wikipedia cannot be marked consensus at all — the validator refuses it — and how many language editions have an article changes nothing about how any of it is drawn.

Licences

Several, because one licence cannot honestly cover code, prose, two imported datasets and two typefaces.

  • The code — everything under src/, tools/ and tests/ — is MIT. CC BY-SA on JavaScript is a nuisance nobody wants.
  • The records — every event, edge, source, place, relation, narrative and actor that a person wrote, which is to say all the writing — are CC BY-SA 4.0. Use them, including commercially, with attribution, and share what you build on them under the same terms.
  • The coastlines and lane polygons under data/geo/ derive from Natural Earth, which is public domain; its makers ask to be credited and are, here and in data/geo/LICENSE.
  • The territories — data/geo/presences/, data/presences/ and the actor records the CShapes import created — are CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, because CShapes 2.0 is. That licence forbids commercial use and the rest of the data does not, so the two are kept apart and each record says which one it carries. Attribution and the full citation are in data/geo/LICENSE.
  • The identifiers — the Wikidata item, the article titles and the count of language editions that a record may carry — are read off Wikidata, whose data is released under CC0 1.0. They stay CC BY-SA 4.0 as part of the record they sit on, which CC0 allows. No Wikipedia prose reaches this site or any record: the one place the repository keeps any is tools/import/cache/, where the import stores an article's opening paragraphs with the revision they came from and a link to the history that credits whoever wrote them — CC BY-SA 4.0, the same licence, and removed before the site is published. The head of data/LICENSE says all of this again.
  • The two typefaces — EB Garamond for what the atlas says and Public Sans for what the interface says — are under the SIL Open Font License 1.1, which is neither the MIT of the code nor the CC BY-SA of the records. The files are in src/fonts/ with their licences beside them and are served from this site rather than from a font CDN, so opening a page tells nobody but the host that you did.

Why this cannot be changed later

Relicensing needs the permission of everyone who holds copyright in the thing being relicensed. The moment a stranger's paragraph is merged, that set includes them, and it grows with every contribution. There is no realistic point after which all of those people can be found and asked. So the licence is not a default that can be revisited when it becomes inconvenient: it is a decision taken once, before contributions opened, and it is final. The same reasoning is why imported geometry under an incompatible licence lives in its own directory with its own attribution and is never merged into a record somebody wrote — which is exactly what happened with CShapes, whose non-commercial clause CC BY-SA cannot absorb in either direction.

Contributions

Not yet open. The schema is still being tested against the first period, and migrating a hundred contributors' records is far harder than migrating your own — so the pipeline is built and tested before it is announced, not after.

When it opens, it will work like this. A form builds a bundle: an event, the edges that connect it and any source not already in the bibliography, validated in your browser against the records already here, so that references resolve and the arrow of time holds before you send anything. The form copies the bundle to your clipboard and opens one issue. A maintainer reads that issue; only when they accept it does it become a pull request, and a person still reviews the argument, the sources and the confidence before it is merged. No sources, no merge. The repository is the database, GitHub is the plumbing, and the judgement stays human — that is not a limitation to engineer away, it is the design.

The reasoning behind all of this is in CONTEXT.md in the repository; the structure is in ARCHITECTURE.md; what is built so far is in STATUS.md.
